Where to Inject Tesamorelin

Research reference · Updated November 2026 · ~7 min read

Tesamorelin is a synthetic growth hormone-releasing factor analog, a 44-amino-acid peptide with a trans-3-hexenoic acid modification that slows breakdown by DPP-4. The only licensed product, EGRIFTA SV, is labeled for subcutaneous injection, and the prescribing information names one region: the abdomen. This page summarizes that administration record for research reference. It is not a guide to injecting research compounds, and it is not medical advice.

What the product label specifies

EGRIFTA SV is tesamorelin for injection, for subcutaneous use. The labeled dose is 1.4 mg, which is 0.35 mL of the reconstituted solution, given once daily. Section 2 of the prescribing information says to inject into the abdomen and to rotate sites. The instructions for use add a distance rule: choose a spot at least 2 inches (5 cm) from the belly button, inside the abdominal area shown on the product diagram.

The same instructions list places to skip. Do not use scar tissue, bruises, or the navel. Avoid spots that still have a hard bump from an earlier injection. The described route is under the skin of the stomach area. Intramuscular and intravenous placement are not the labeled route, and the thigh or upper arm is not the region named in the instructions.

These lines describe the licensed kit. They are not a dosing recommendation for research vials, which are a different presentation. Figures discussed in the research literature are collected separately on the tesamorelin dosage page.

Why the abdomen, and why rotation

The HIV lipodystrophy trials that supported approval used daily subcutaneous tesamorelin, and the label kept that site. The indication is reduction of excess abdominal fat in adults with HIV and lipodystrophy, not general weight loss. The label states it is not indicated for weight-loss management. Placing the injection in abdominal subcutaneous tissue matches how the drug was studied. It does not mean other subcutaneous regions were proven equivalent.

Rotation is there because local reactions are common. In the first 26 weeks of the clinical trials, injection site reactions occurred in 25% of tesamorelin-treated patients and 14% of placebo-treated patients. The warnings section lists erythema, pruritus, pain, irritation, and bruising. Reactions reported at more than 5% also include injection site erythema and injection site pruritus, alongside arthralgia, pain in extremity, peripheral edema, and myalgia. The label advice is to move the site around the abdomen to lower that risk. A broader map of how research peptides are placed under the skin is in the peptide injection sites guide.

Systemic effects are a separate question from the local reaction. Fluid retention, rising IGF-1, and glucose changes are discussed in the tesamorelin side effects overview. Site choice does not remove those signals.

How this differs from other research peptides

Ipamorelin and CJC-1295 are also discussed as subcutaneous research peptides, but they are not tesamorelin and they do not inherit the EGRIFTA abdominal diagram. Mechanism notes for ipamorelin sit on the ipamorelin protocol page. Pairing another peptide on the same day does not move the labeled tesamorelin site.

BPC-157 and TB-500 notes often mention placement near a tissue of interest. That habit does not transfer here. Tesamorelin is a systemic GHRH analog, and the published instructions stay on the abdomen. Visceral fat sits deeper than the layer a subcutaneous needle enters, so changing the spot is not how those trials measured it.

Purity still changes the local picture: redness, itching, or a lump can come from excipients or contaminants rather than tesamorelin itself. Research vials are not the preservative-free EGRIFTA SV kit. Material with third-party HPLC and mass-spec testing is the practical check. Sourcing notes are on the supplier list.

Reconstitution is not the same as site

Site and volume get mixed together in search queries. For the licensed EGRIFTA SV 2 mg vial, the label says to add 0.5 mL of the supplied sterile water for injection, roll the vial gently for 30 seconds, and not shake it. The daily amount withdrawn is 0.35 mL (1.4 mg). Unused solution is discarded. The solution should be clear and colorless, with no particles.

That math belongs to the 2 mg EGRIFTA SV kit only. The older 1 mg-per-vial EGRIFTA product used a 2 mg daily dose, a different number of vials, and different storage rules. The label warns that the two formulations are not interchangeable. Research lyophilized vials are not either kit. They may be filled at 2 mg, 5 mg, 10 mg, or another amount, and they are often paired with bacteriostatic water rather than the single-use sterile water in the commercial box. Copying 0.35 mL onto a research vial is a concentration error. The reconstitution calculator turns vial mass and diluent volume into a concentration so the two products are not treated as the same solution.

What a documented site still does not settle

An abdominal site on a label does not turn a research compound into EGRIFTA SV. The HIV-lipodystrophy indication is narrow, long-term cardiovascular benefit was not established, and pregnancy is a labeled contraindication for the drug product. Research material can also differ in purity, salt form, and excipients.

If a study design includes a second peptide on the same day, handling notes keep the deposits in different spots. Overlapping wheals make it impossible to tell which compound caused a reaction.
